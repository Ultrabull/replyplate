Diners have already decided that it does. Nine in ten expect a business to reply, and four in ten say they will not use one that never does. It is not about the star rating. It is that a reply is the only proof a stranger has that somebody is paying attention.

Almost nobody does, and the reason is the clock, not the will. A fifth of people now expect an answer the same day, a third by the next, and four in five within the week. Before you buy, the honest version

Every diner, not just the happy ones.

Everyone sees the same QR code. Filtering to only the happy ones breaks Google's rules, and the FTC treats review suppression as deceptive, so we will not do it.

We will not promise a better rating.

Nobody honest will. Google will even draft replies free on some accounts and the writing is fine, but it still needs you to log in and press send, every time, forever.

Google reviews only.

Not Yelp, not TripAdvisor, not OpenTable. Facebook retired star ratings, so no tool can reply to those. We do handle your Facebook and Instagram posts.

Anything negative waits for you.

It reaches your phone with a calm reply already written and waits. There is no setting that turns this off. Anything already posted can be edited or deleted from your Google profile at any time, by you or by us.

AI writes the first version. A person checks it.

Nothing posts under an invented employee name. Replies go out as your restaurant, because that is whose name is on them.

A person reads every review. That is the point.

ReplyPlate is run by one person, and a good part of the work is done by hand. That is deliberate. It is why nothing goes out sounding like a form letter, and why an angry review meets a human before it meets your customers.

The honest trade: your reviews get worked every business day, not within a minute of landing. If you want a robot that answers in ten seconds, Google will do that free. We think the ten seconds is the least valuable part.

We will not text your diners.

Texting strangers in the US carries damages of hundreds of dollars a message. We ask with a printed code instead, and a code left in the office drawer gets you nothing, which is why we push receipts first.

If you leave, you keep the work.

Every reply we posted stays up, in your name, and we never take them down. Your review code keeps working forever, because it points at Google and not at us. We come off your Google profile the same day you say so. Nothing you paid for gets switched off behind you.

Where the $199 comes back, honestly

Everybody selling you something like this waves one flattering number at you. Here are all four places the money comes from, including the one that is smaller than you would hope.

  1. $10 to $12 A delivery order that becomes a pickup order Somebody about to tap DoorDash sees your own link first, and a free side for using it. On a $40 order the delivery commission you never paid is around a quarter of it. This is the big one, and the offer is what makes people switch.
  2. The whole margin An order that would not have happened at all The phone that rings at 7:40 on a Friday when every pair of hands is carrying plates. A caller who gets no answer does not sit there waiting for one. The chat answers, takes the order, and texts it to you. Nobody can put a number on how often that happens in your place, so we will not pretend to.
  3. About $2.80 A pickup order moved off an app Smaller than everyone tells you. The apps take 6 to 7 percent on pickup, not the 25 to 30 they take on delivery. On a $40 order that is under three dollars, so you would need roughly seventy of them a month for this line alone to cover the fee. It will not, on its own, and anybody telling you otherwise is quoting you a delivery rate for a pickup order.
  4. The rest Every review answered, every diner asked, eight posts written Which is most of what you are actually buying. The ordering is the part that shows up as money. The reviews are the part that decides whether somebody walks in at all.
